Chapter 10. Outliers, hubs, and abstention

Data Mining as Observation, draft 0.3, 2026-09-09. Week 10 of ECE 514. Reading due before this chapter is TSK 9.1 to 9.4 [1]. Section 0.16 of the toolkit introduces the outlier vocabulary this chapter uses. Every number below traces to a committed file listed in the last section.

An outlier is a row that does not fit. TSK's chapter offers four ways to say what fit means. A row is far from the centre in standard-deviation units. A row is far from its nearest neighbours. A row sits in a region less dense than its neighbours' regions. A row is far from every cluster centre. Each is a reasonable definition, and the four disagree about which rows are outliers.

This chapter says why they disagree and what to do about it. Each definition is a reader, and an outlier is a row the reader cannot place. Then it turns to the outliers that matter most in modern practice, the rows of an embedding corpus that no query ever retrieves, and shows that they come in five kinds, that one kind is manufactured by the query budget, and that the remedy depends on the mechanism. It ends with the rule that governs every report of a tail. Take the verdict as the worst group, count the groups too thin to judge, and never let a mean hide either.

10.1 An outlier is a row the reader cannot place

Write each of the week's detectors as an observer.

The statistical detector scores a row by its distance from the mean in units of spread. In one dimension that is the z-score. In many it is the Mahalanobis distance of equation 0.33, the Euclidean distance after whitening. This reader is the identity reader of chapter 4 applied to whitened data. It treats every whitened direction equally and calls a row an outlier when the sum of its squared whitened coordinates is large.

$$z(x)=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma},\qquad d_M(x)^{2}=(x-\mu)^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}(x-\mu)=\sum_i\frac{(v_i\cdot(x-\mu))^{2}}{\lambda_i}. \tag{10.1}$$

The proximity detector scores a row by the distance to its k-th nearest neighbour. It reads whatever the distance reads, and chapter 3 said a distance is a choice of what to ignore. Under cosine it cannot see a row that is far from everything in length but close in angle.

The density detector scores a row by the ratio of its neighbours' local density to its own [36]. A row is an outlier when it is much sparser than the rows around it, which lets a detector find outliers in a sparse cluster without calling the whole cluster outlying. This reader reads the density coordinate, the one chapter 3's geodesic reader discards and chapter 9's DBSCAN reads.

$$\mathrm{LOF}(x)=\frac{\frac1k\sum_{y\in N_k(x)}\mathrm{dens}(y)}{\mathrm{dens}(x)},\qquad \mathrm{dens}(x)=\Big(\frac1k\sum_{y\in N_k(x)}d(x,y)\Big)^{-1}. \tag{10.2}$$

The clustering detector scores a row by its distance to the nearest cluster centre, and inherits the reader of whichever clustering produced the centres.

Four readers, four read subspaces, four sets of outliers. A row far along a high-variance direction is an outlier to the proximity detector and not to the statistical one. A row in a sparse cluster is an outlier to the proximity detector and not to the density one. The session deck distinguishes outliers from anomalies, and the observer version of that distinction is that an anomaly is an outlier to the reader that will act on it. A fraud detector's outliers are the rows the fraud consumer cannot place. Any other reader's outliers are a different list.

That the z-score is unchanged under a joint rescaling and shift, that a row passes the threshold exactly when it lies more than that many spreads from the mean, and that the weight beyond a threshold is at most the mean squared score over the threshold squared, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

Two consequences follow. A report names the reader before it names the outliers. And because outlier detection has no labels, its validity index needs a null, and chapter 8 section 8.4 gives the rule. The number of outliers a detector finds in data with no outliers is the number it will find anyway.

10.2 Anti-hubs are the observer's outliers

Chapter 3 introduced hubs [19]. Count, for every row of a corpus, how many queries' top-k lists it appears in. A few rows appear in hundreds. A large fraction appear in none. Those never-retrieved rows are the anti-hubs, and chapter 11 said they are where compressed indexes fail first, because quantization rounds fine distinctions away and the rows that are found by fine distinctions are found no more. Aggregate recall barely moves, because they are a minority.

$$N_k(x\mid Q)=\big|\{q\in Q:\ x\in\operatorname{top}_k(q)\}\big|,\qquad \text{anti-hub}:\ N_k(x)=0. \tag{10.3}$$

That the rows with count above a ceiling number at most the slots handed out over the ceiling plus one, so that hubs are few by arithmetic, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

That the counts sum to the slots handed out, that they depend on the queries alone once the retrieval rule is fixed, and that a row is an anti-hub exactly when no query retrieves it, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

Anti-hubs are outliers, and they are outliers to a specific reader, the query workload. The vector-search benchmark of chapter 11 asked whether they are one thing or several [16], and registered the answer before measuring it.

Five kinds. Every row never retrieved at k of 10 was assigned one of five mechanisms of invisibility by a latent rule. It was never asked for, meaning no query's true neighbourhood came near it. It sits in low density. It sits on a boundary between regions. Its metric does not fit, meaning it is close under the true relation and far under the distance. Or it is a legitimate anti-hub, isolated under every reading. A deliberately weak nearest-centroid classifier was then asked to recover the category from geometry and query exposure alone, on held-out rows, without the rule.

Balanced accuracy, equation 0.34, cleared the registered 0.6 at every corpus size and reached 0.718 at the harness convention, with every category recovered above chance. The categories are not an arbitrary partition of the tail. They are recoverable from geometry and query exposure.

The sweep is itself a result. When queries cover the corpus generously, at 3.34 slots per row, half of all invisible rows are invisible because nothing asked for them. As coverage falls, that category deflates monotonically, 0.499 to 0.327 to 0.257, while the legitimate category inflates, 0.309 to 0.409 to 0.434, because with too few queries the pigeonhole floor manufactures anti-hubs that are nothing of the kind. At half a slot per row, at least half the corpus cannot be retrieved by arithmetic. A tail statistic measured at one query budget is partly a property of that budget, and a generator matched on it would be matched on an artefact.

$$\text{never retrieved}\ \ge\ 1-\frac{|Q|\,k}{n}\qquad\text{whenever}\quad |Q|\,k

The half that failed. The same registration predicted that the benchmark's hubness gate, the skewness of the count distribution, would be blind to the category mix, so that a lower-tail discriminator would be a missing axis of the battery. Subsample pairs matched on that gate differed in category proportions by at most 1.28 times against a registered threshold of 2. The gate is not blind. The instrument claim was withdrawn, per the registered clause, and the withdrawal was scoped. The matching was by rejection within one corpus, so the pairs were similar in many ways beyond the gate, and the stronger test across structurally different corpora was not run. The taxonomy stands as a descriptive instrument with a measured budget dependence, and not as evidence of a missing gate.

For a project the reading is three sentences. The rows a system never retrieves are its outliers. They come in kinds, and the kinds have different causes. Count the query budget before counting the tail.

10.3 The mechanism decides the remedy

Chapter 3 showed two corpora with the same hubness skewness and different hubs, one driven by local density and one by centrality. The compression library's anatomy tool classifies each hub by mechanism [12] and prescribes a remedy, and its design history is the chapter's lesson about measuring outliers.

A hub is typed hierarchically. If its centrality sits in the population's top five percent, it is a centrality hub regardless of its density, because a point close to everything mechanically has a small neighbour distance, so apparent density is implied by centrality and carries no independent information. Only among the non-central hubs is density diagnosed, at the top fifteen percent of the population's density distribution. The fractions of a corpus's hubs of each type then choose the prescription.

$$\begin{gathered} \text{central}:\ \mathrm{pct}_{\mathrm{centrality}}\ge0.95; \\ \text{dense}:\ \neg\text{central}\ \wedge\ \mathrm{pct}_{\mathrm{density}}\ge0.85; \qquad \text{prescribe by }f_{\mathrm{central}},\ f_{\mathrm{dense}}\ \text{at}\ 0.75. \end{gathered} \tag{10.5}$$

Centrality hubs call for centering the corpus, and a check of the pipeline for a mean shift or a collapsed subspace upstream. Density hubs call for mutual-proximity rescaling, which needs one precomputed scalar per row. A mixed corpus gets both, centre first. A corpus with no dominant mechanism gets the instruction to re-measure with real queries. And when the busiest row's count is below two and a half times k, the tool abstains from attributing any mechanism, because noise hubs get no prescription.

Two designs that died in the tests. The first design classified by the corpus-wide correlation between a row's count and its neighbour distance. That correlation exceeds 0.8 on an isotropic Gaussian, where there is no hub structure at all, because a point with close neighbours symmetrically tends to appear in their lists. The design prescribed density rescaling to every corpus. The second design used symmetric percentile thresholds for centrality and density, and misfiled every centrality hub as mixed, because centrality implies density. Both died in the classifier's own confusion-matrix fixtures, and the hierarchical rule is what survived. The thresholds are visible in the source and quoted in every report as heuristics, so the classification states its own inputs.

The general lesson is chapter 2's. A correction of the wrong shape passes a test that only checks one number. A corpus-wide correlation is one number, and it was rank-coupled to the count in every corpus. The remedy needed a per-hub reading.

10.4 Trust the tail, not the mean

A global scalar hides regional pathology. A corpus with recall 0.98 can have one language, one product category, or one time window at 0.6, and the mean will not say so. The compression library's stratified specification partitions measurement by area [12] and takes every verdict as the minimum over areas, with three rules that a data-mining report can adopt directly.

The area map is part of the measurement. Rows and queries are assigned to areas, either by a clustering computed and fingerprinted, or by a declared metadata key such as language. Nearest neighbours ignore borders, so boundary rows are assigned to more than one area, or queries probe their home area and its neighbours, and a configuration that does neither cannot be certified. The map has a content hash, and two artifacts computed under different maps must not be compared, merged, or gated together. Tools refuse rather than warn.

Per-area counts separate local hubs from transit. A row's count splits into the queries from its own area and the queries from elsewhere, and the transit fraction is the share from elsewhere.

$$N_k^{\mathrm{intra}}(x)=N_k\big(x\mid\{q:\ a_Q(q)\cap a(x)\ne\emptyset\}\big),\qquad \tau(x)=\frac{N_k(x)-N_k^{\mathrm{intra}}(x)}{\max(N_k(x),1)}. \tag{10.6}$$

An area whose hubs have high counts and low transit is a local-hub area, with a name borrowed from routing and kept because it is accurate. An area whose rows carry high transit is a backbone area. An area with low counts in and out is a stub.

Thin strata abstain. A per-area verdict requires a minimum number of rows and a minimum number of queries. Below either, the area's verdict is abstain, which is reported, counted under a registered cause, and excluded from the pass or fail aggregation. Abstention is not a pass. Skewness on a thin stratum is noise, and uncertain means no verdict.

$$\text{verdict}=\min_{i:\ n_i\ge n_{\min},\ |Q_i|\ge q_{\min}}\ \mathrm{score}_i,\qquad \text{ABSTAIN otherwise}. \tag{10.7}$$

That abstain is not a pass, that the verdict is abstain exactly when no stratum is eligible, and that raising the eligibility bar can only raise the verdict, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

That the verdict passes a bar exactly when every scored stratum passes it, and that it never exceeds a weighted mean of the strata, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The first measured run, and its two wrong predictions. The specification's first stratified run took a 350000-row sample of a 2.4-million-row multilingual corpus, embedded with a general encoder, with language as the area key. Of fourteen languages, seven were eligible and seven abstained, among them a language with two rows and three with one. Two predictions had been frozen before any stratified measurement existed. The first, that per-language hubness would vary widely, with a Robin Hood ratio of at least 1.5 between the most and least hubby language, measured 1.30, with seven languages spanning 63 times in sample size, three scripts, and three millennia of text landing in a band of width 0.10. Not confirmed. The record's sentence is that the reviewer eats it publicly, as promised. The second, that a translation-trained encoder concentrates cross-lingual transit on a central core, inverted on both legs. The trained encoder produced more transit, 0.389 against 0.291, carried by rows less central than the population, spread less concentratedly, and it turned seven of thirteen eligible languages into backbone areas, the first the instrument had ever measured, while the general encoder kept every language a local-hub area. Training an interlingua does not build a core. It dissolves the borders. Both predictions were wrong in the informative direction, and the specification's own taxonomy class that had been speculative at design time was found to exist.

For a project the rule is one line. Report the tail by stratum, take the minimum, count the abstentions, and print the mean only beside them.

10.5 A remedy applied where it cannot work

The density remedy of section 10.3, mutual-proximity rescaling, is known to reduce hubness. The question the library then asked under seal was whether it survives compression, and the answer came as a ladder of gates in which three of the four exist because an earlier one failed. The record typesets the failures like passes.

Gate A failed by design error. The rescored rankings from compressed codes were graded against the uncorrected exact ranking as truth. Anti-hub recall dropped about 0.32 in every stratum. The diagnosis was that the gate had conflated two questions. The rescaling is a different ranking objective, so scoring it against the old truth measures disagreement, not damage. The gate design failed before the remedy could. It stays on the scoreboard as the reason the next gate exists.

The two questions separated. Does the remedy reduce hubness on the exact graph? Yes. On 350000 rows, count skewness fell from 3.970 to 3.177, the busiest count from 287 to 213, the Robin Hood index from 0.372 to 0.261, and the fraction of rows with count above twice k from 0.117 to 0.079. Does the compressed path reproduce the corrected ranking? No. Minimum-over-strata anti-hub recall against the corrected truth was 0.663 against a bar of 0.90, every one of seven strata failed, and the corrected ranking was harder to reproduce from compressed codes than the uncorrected one, 0.62 to 0.69 against 0.76 to 0.84. The remedy promotes exactly the isolated rows that 27.7 times compression damages first.

Moving the remedy did nothing, and that was the finding. Applying the rescaling at a rerank stage instead of in compressed scoring gave minimum-over-strata anti-hub recall of 0.6627, identical to the previous gate to four decimals. The bottleneck was not the scoring layer. It was candidate coverage. The corrected ranking's true neighbours often never appeared in the compressed top 51 at all, and no rerank can recover a candidate that was never fetched.

Deeper candidates saturate. Sweeping candidate depth from 51 to 501, coverage of the corrected truth climbed from 0.696 to 0.926 while fidelity climbed from 0.663 to 0.702 and flattened. Past a depth near 100 the ceiling was score precision. The right neighbour was in the list and the compressed scores were too coarse to place it. Ten times the scan depth bought 0.039.

Paying for it works. Reranking against stored full-precision originals, at a cost of 4096 bytes per row on top of 148, reached fidelity 0.923 at depth 501, equal to coverage at every depth as predicted, and cleared the 0.90 bar in every stratum. The codes find and the originals decide.

A bit allocation that dug under the floor. A separate gate tried to raise the minimum stratum by giving more bits to the fragile strata. It raised all eight targets by 0.004 to 0.034 and cut the unprotected donors below the old floor, 0.7118 against a baseline minimum of 0.7251. It flattened the distribution and lowered the minimum. A redesigned allocator that let only strata with headroom donate passed. Minimum-over-strata is not improved by spending on the weak. It is improved by not digging under the floor.

That a rerank's recall is at most the candidate coverage, and that a deeper candidate list can only raise the coverage, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The ladder is chapter 8's discipline applied to an outlier remedy. Every gate was declared before it ran, every failure was diagnosed rather than hidden, and the deployment guidance that came out is precise. The remedy works at the exact layer for free, fails three ways in the compressed path, and works in the compressed path only when paid for with stored originals.

10.6 What an outlier report must state

A project report that detects outliers or anomalies states the following beside its list.

  • The reader. Which of the four detectors, and the consumer that will act on the outliers, with the reason the two match.
  • The null. The number of outliers the detector finds on data with no outliers, obtained by shuffling or by derivation.
  • The query budget, if the outliers are anti-hubs, as slots per row, with the pigeonhole floor stated.
  • Anti-hub recall and fifth-percentile per-query recall beside mean recall, for any index.
  • The strata, with the minimum, the count of abstentions, and the map's hash.
  • The mechanism and the remedy, and the layer at which the remedy was applied and tested.

A report that presents a mean and a threshold will be returned.

Exercises

Warm-up

  1. Write the four TSK detectors as observer triples and name what each reads.
  2. Explain why a row can be an outlier to the proximity detector and not to the statistical one.
  3. State the difference between an outlier and an anomaly in the book's terms.
  4. Why is the fraction of rows never retrieved at half a slot per row at least one half?
  5. Name the five kinds of anti-hub and say which one the query budget manufactures.
  6. Why was the instrument claim in section 10.2 withdrawn, and how was the withdrawal scoped?
  7. Explain why a corpus-wide correlation between count and neighbour distance cannot identify the hub mechanism.
  8. Why is a hub with centrality in the top five percent typed as central regardless of its density?
  9. State the abstention rule and say why abstention is neither a pass nor a fail.
  10. Both predictions of the first stratified run were wrong. Say in one sentence each what was learned from each.
  11. Why did moving the remedy to the rerank stage change nothing, and what did that identify?
  12. Why did the fragile-first allocation lower the minimum it was trying to raise?

By hand

  1. A dataset has mean zero and covariance diagonal (1, 4). Compute the Euclidean and Mahalanobis distances of the rows (3, 4) and (4, 3), and state which detector calls which row more outlying.
  2. Five rows have retrieval counts 10, 6, 2, 2, and 0. Compute the Robin Hood index, equation 0.35, and the fraction of rows that are anti-hubs.
  3. One thousand queries each retrieve ten neighbours from a corpus of 20000 rows. Compute slots per row and the pigeonhole floor on the never-retrieved fraction. The measured fraction was 0.759. State the fraction attributable to the floor.
  4. A classifier recovers 80 of 100 rows of one class and 30 of 50 of another. Compute accuracy and balanced accuracy.
  5. Four strata score anti-hub recall 0.94, 0.88, and 0.71, and the fourth abstains. Compute the mean of the three scored, the min-over-strata verdict, and state whether a bar of 0.90 passes.
  6. A count distribution has skewness 3.970 before a remedy and 3.177 after, and the busiest count falls from 287 to 213. Compute both relative reductions.
  7. Candidate depth rises from 51 to 501, coverage from 0.696 to 0.926, and fidelity from 0.663 to 0.702. Compute the fidelity gained per unit of coverage gained, and state what limits fidelity at depth 501.

Programming

  1. Run all four TSK detectors on your project dataset and report the ten most outlying rows under each. Report the overlap between the four lists.
  2. Shuffle each column of your dataset independently and rerun the four detectors. Report the number of rows each flags at your chosen threshold. That is the null.
  3. Compute neighbour counts for your corpus under your real query set at k of 10, following section 0.9's function. Report slots per row, the never-retrieved fraction, and the pigeonhole floor.
  4. Classify your never-retrieved rows into the five kinds using the rules of section 10.2, a row's distance to the nearest query, its local density, its distance to the nearest region boundary, and its rank under an alternative metric. Report the mix. Halve the query set and report the mix again.
  5. Type your top twenty hubs by percentile in centrality and density with the hierarchy of equation 10.5, and report the prescription.
  6. Partition your corpus into strata by a metadata key or by k-means with at least eight cells. Compute anti-hub recall per stratum for your index, apply the abstention rule with a stated minimum, and report the minimum, the mean, and the abstention count.
  7. Apply mutual-proximity rescaling on the exact graph and report the change in count skewness and busiest count. Then apply it in your index's compressed path and report anti-hub recall against the corrected truth, per stratum.

Answers to the warm-up and by-hand problems are collected in the answer key at the end of the book.
