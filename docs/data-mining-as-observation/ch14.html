Chapter 14. Ethics, interpretability, and deployment as observation

Data Mining as Observation, draft 0.3, 2026-09-09. Week 14 of ECE 514. Reading due before this chapter is TSK 10.1 to 10.2 [1], with the rest of TSK 10 skimmed. Section 0.18 of the toolkit introduces the deployment vocabulary this chapter uses. Every number below traces to a committed file listed in the last section.

The session deck opens the last lecture with three ways to be confidently wrong at deployment time, and the book has spent thirteen chapters naming them. The metric was not the consumer's. The harness read something the deployed consumer will not. The certificate aged. This chapter takes the deck's four topics, rules against embeddings, fairness, interpretability, and monitoring, and reads each as an observation problem, with one worked instrument running through all four.

The instrument is a content-moderation system that reads a decision as a spectrum across ten moral axes [35, 32] rather than as one toxicity score. It was built to answer three questions a scalar cannot. Which value did the decision act on. Does the decision move when the same sentence is reworded. What does the instrument fail to see. Every number below is from its record, and the record carries its failures at the same size as its results, including one axis that failed validation twice, one candidate axis, threat, flagged by an unbalanced pass and retracted by the balanced one before any admission, a refusal hole of 24 percent, and an adversarial test the mechanism did not pass.

14.1 A decision is a spectrum, and every axis is validated or it does not vote

A scalar toxicity score collapses a morally significant decision into one number. That number cannot say which value it acted on, it drifts when the sentence is reworded, and it cannot say what it fails to see. The instrument decomposes the signal into one score per axis, each produced by its own small encoder, each validated by the cross-corpus gate of chapter 12 or flagged as unvalidated. An unvalidated encoder cannot vote. The rule is enforced in code, not in policy. A scored axis is bound to its validation record inside a hash-chained audit artifact, so a decision can never silently rest on an encoder that failed its gate.

The instrument's answer is that a rule is the right consumer for an axis whose encoder cannot pass the gate, and that a validated encoder is the right consumer for the rest, and that both must show their validation record.

14.2 Authority is calibrated

A binary validated bit treats an encoder at AUROC 0.63 and one at 0.85 as equal voters. The instrument's reviewer called that laundering a threefold reliability difference into equal authority, and the fix was equation 0.38. Each axis's score enters the decision weighted by its reliability, and the collapsed family of axes that chapter 12 found to be one general channel enters as a single factor carrying the mean of its members' weights, 0.559. The general valence channel's own gate weight, 0.735, is the reliability of the rival channel that absorbed those axes, and it is not the weight the collapsed factor votes with, since the design rule gives the factor its members' mean rather than the channel's.

$$S=\frac{\sum_i w_i\,s_i}{\sum_i w_i},\qquad w_i=\max\big(0,\ 2\cdot\mathrm{AUROC}_i-1\big). \tag{14.1}$$

Three design rules travel with the weights. The weight tempers authority and never transparency, so a low-reliability axis still appears in the decision's residue as considered and discarded. The calibration error is measured on a held-out half of the validation pairs, because an in-sample isotonic fit has error near zero by construction and was rejected. And the escalation trigger, the threshold at which an item is handed to a person, was left on raw confidence, because its threshold had been registered against raw confidences, and changing its meaning silently is exactly the unregistered bar move that chapter 8 forbids. If a future round wants the trigger weighted, that is a registered re-gate, not a code edit.

Two non-improvements are reported as measured. Physical harm was already calibrated and moved from 0.048 to 0.049. The environmental axis got slightly worse on the held-out half, from 0.089 to 0.101, a small-sample overfit at 696 pairs.

14.3 The instrument measures its own coverage and finds what it is missing

A fixed taxonomy is imposed, never tested. The instrument tests its own by asking, for each category a moderation dataset labels, how much of the category the validated axes can read compared to the raw embedding they were built from. The difference is signal the current axes miss, and because a higher-dimensional embedding always carries some extra signal, the difference on categories the axes do cover is the empirical noise floor. A real missing dimension has to clear it.

$$\mathrm{coverage\ difference}_c=\mathrm{AUROC}_c(\text{embedding})-\mathrm{AUROC}_c(\text{validated axes}),\qquad \text{floor}\approx0.08\ \text{to}\ 0.12. \tag{14.2}$$

On a balanced resample of 3001 items with 250 to 300 positives per rare category and a bootstrap interval, two categories cleared the floor by two to three times. Identity attack, at 0.237 with interval 0.20 to 0.28 on 289 positives, where the eight axes reached only 0.750 and the embedding 0.987. Sexual content, at 0.204. Threat, at 0.093, sat at the floor. A first unbalanced pass with eighteen positives had overstated threat and flagged it. The balanced confirmation retracted it. The protocol caught its own false positive.

Clearing the floor says that signal exists outside the axes. Whether it is a moral dimension is a separate admission question, and the two candidates were answered differently. Sexual content was declined, because in the corpus it is 87.7 percent toxic and 1.4 percent non-toxic, so its moral weight is harassment, which the existing axes cover, and consensual explicitness is not a moral violation, so the sign is not moral. It was routed to a separate policy channel outside the moral tensor. Identity attack was admitted, and then built and held to the identical preregistered gate the other eight had faced.

The discovered axis, gated. Two independent corpora, a civil-comments set with 1400 rows and a hate-speech set with 5000, balanced to 3200 against 3200. The untrained and bag-of-words nulls were measured on this corpus and frozen into the bar before training, so that the baseline could not be calibrated to bless the result. The corpus-construction choices necessarily post-dated the discovery, and the record says so, and says why that is not a thumb on the scale. Any lexical or sampling artifact those choices introduce is available to the nulls too.

Two caveats are printed beside the pass in the record and are printed here. And a domain-classifier accuracy of zero in the report is a sampling artifact of which rows the probe read, not evidence of de-confounding. The load-bearing numbers are 0.80 and 0.25.

The step not yet taken. A blind recovery experiment was registered with every knob frozen, the ridge penalty, the component count, the stability bar of 0.70, the cross-corpus bar of 0.60, the non-redundancy bar of 0.35, the unblinding bar of 0.70, and a selection correction that tests only the single pre-designated highest-stability survivor or else a permutation null, because testing every survivor and calling any pass a recovery is a softer bar than it looks. It asks whether the unsupervised pipeline can recover the known axis without being shown its label. The record names its blindness as procedural rather than epistemic, because the axis was put there. The experiment had not been run when this chapter was written, and its registered limitation is the chapter's. The embedding is the new ceiling. A distinction invisible to the embedding is invisible to the residual.

14.4 The verdict must survive re-description

A decision that moves when the sentence is reworded has read the wording, not the meaning. The instrument's mechanism is to generate a class of meaning-preserving rewordings, score each, and decide on the average. Its measure of invariance is the ratio of decision movement within a class to movement between unrelated inputs.

$$\theta_d=\frac{\operatorname{mean}_i\ \big|S_A(i)-S_B(i)\big|}{\operatorname{mean}_{i\ne j}\ \big|S_A(i)-S_A(j)\big|},\qquad \text{bar}\ \theta_d\le0.5. \tag{14.3}$$

That the ratio is nonnegative, zero exactly when no item's score moves under rewording, and at most one half exactly when the movement is at most half the spread between inputs, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The two halves of each class are scored separately, so the numerator is a conservative estimate of the deployed residual. The bar had been calibrated on eight synthetic scenarios, which the record calls selection evidence, and the at-scale protocol was registered before running on sixty fresh items excluded by hash from every training set.

Both clear the bar, and both carry caveats that the record says must be quoted beside them. The aligned model refused to reword 19 of 79 sampled items, 24 percent, and refused exactly the overtly toxic content on which invariance matters most. A refused item becomes a singleton class, gets no averaging, and is routed to escalation. The back-translation run answered whether the hole was in the mechanism or the generator. The generator refused 20 percent of the harmful items and back-translation refused none, and the mechanism held at 0.301, so the hole is the generator's, and a non-refusing fallback paraphraser is the deployment mitigation.

The negative. The ratio measures score movement. The deployment question is whether a flagged item still slips past the threshold after averaging. On six hand-authored scenarios, each a base text with an audited euphemistic rewrite, three of six flagged items flipped to pass under the raw score and three of six still flipped under class averaging. Averaging trimmed the displacement by 17 percent against 46 percent on natural rewordings. A euphemistic input produces a class of euphemistic rewordings, polite versions of a polite lie, and the average stays polite. Adversarial-register robustness is a distinct target the mechanism does not meet, and the record keeps escalate-by-default load-bearing for that reason. The optimizing-attacker experiment that would measure it stays registered and unrun.

Cross-lingual invariance was measured at scale on 60 items in five languages, half harmful, and the index read 0.721 with interval 0.71 to 0.74 for the general encoder and 0.804 for the translation-trained one, with harmful and benign items within 0.04 of each other under both. Chapter 12 said which encoder to expect to win and why.

14.5 Interpretability is an estimate of the read operator

The deck's interpretability toolkit is importances, partial dependence, and Shapley-style attributions [8], with their uses and abuses. The book's account is two sentences. A gradient-based attribution is an estimate of the consumer's sensitivity of section 0.5, at a point or averaged, and the read operator of equation 0.9 is the object it estimates. The other methods in the toolkit measure something else, and the section says what.

$$\text{attribution}_j(x)\approx g_j(x)\,\delta_j,\qquad \overline{\text{importance}}_j\approx\big(P_C\big)_{jj}=\mathbb E\big[g_j^{2}\big]. \tag{14.4}$$

Those sentences explain the abuses. An averaged gradient importance is approximately a diagonal entry of the read operator, the expected squared sensitivity to one feature. The off-diagonal entries are co-sensitivities, how two features' sensitivities vary together across rows, and they are not interactions. A linear model has no interactions and a dense read operator. An interaction is a cross-partial, the change in one feature's sensitivity when another feature moves, and it lives in the Hessian of the consumer or in a functional decomposition of it, not in the read operator, so no estimate of the read operator can see one. Only gradient-based methods relate directly to the read operator. A permutation importance measures the loss from breaking a feature's joint distribution with the others. A partial dependence plot averages the consumer over rows that may never occur together and reads it at inputs it was never trained on. A Shapley attribution is a weighted average of the consumer's changes over subsets of features, a functional decomposition rather than a derivative, and it can see an interaction that a gradient at one row cannot. A per-row gradient attribution is the consumer's sensitivity at one row and does not transfer to the next. And for a tree, chapter 6 said, the sensitivity is zero almost everywhere, so importances that count splits are the right reading and finite differences are the wrong one.

That an attribution sums exactly to the change in output for an affine consumer, is zero on a coordinate the consumer does not read, and averages to the read operator's diagonal, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The instrument's own interpretability is a formula. Its contraction from ten axes to a toxicity verdict was searched with chapter 6's tool and came out as fairness minus the general valence component, which is low fairness relative to how bad the text is overall. That is a consumer a person can read, and chapter 6 said when a formula suffices. The audit proof behind each decision lists every axis's score, weight, and validation record, and the residue lists the axes considered and discarded, so the decision can be reconstructed by someone who did not make it. That is the deck's auditability and recourse, as objects rather than aspirations.

14.6 Fairness is measured per group, with a null, and cannot be had three ways

The deck covers disparate impact, base rates, and the impossibility results at survey level. The book's reading is chapter 10's. Fairness metrics are per-group output metrics, and a fair report takes the worst group, counts the groups too small to score, and never lets the mean hide either. Each group's false-clear rate is conditional on the clearance, as equation 0.26 defines it, and is reported beside the group's coverage, the fraction of its items the model cleared.

$$\mathrm{FC}_g=\Pr\big[y=\text{violation}\ \big|\ \hat y=\text{clear},\ g\big],\qquad \text{verdict}=\max_{g:\ n_g\ge n_{\min}}\mathrm{FC}_g,\qquad \text{ABSTAIN otherwise}. \tag{14.5}$$

The impossibility result says that when two groups have different base rates [9, 10], a classifier cannot be calibrated within each group and also have equal false-positive and false-negative rates across them, except in degenerate cases. In the book's terms, calibration, equalized odds, and disparate impact are three output metrics, and no single consumer serves all three. A fairness claim therefore names which metric, which groups, the base rate in each, and the count in each, and reports the metric on shuffled group labels as its null. The multilingual run of chapter 10 is the template. Seven of fourteen languages abstained, and the per-language numbers were reported against a prediction that had been wrong.

14.7 Monitoring is the certificate that ages

The deck closes with drift alarms, shadow deployment, and retraining cadence, and chapter 13 gave each its name. A deployed model's benchmark score is a certificate. The deployment traffic is the footprint the certificate cannot see. A drift alarm is a witness, and the number it produces is a false-clear rate per footprint. Chapter 12 section 12.6 had the sealed cell, a model that passed at 0.909 and false-cleared on ten of thirty deployment slices. Shadow deployment is the witness run before the certificate is acted on. Retraining cadence is the refresh interval, and chapter 13 showed that the intuitive proportionality law for it was refuted and that the sealed horizon collapsed.

The instrument's escalation rule is the deployment form of chapter 10's abstention. An item between the thresholds, an item whose paraphrase class the generator refused, an item whose axis failed its gate, is handed to a person. The operating point moderates about 51 percent of items at about 80 percent remove-precision and 95 percent allow-precision on a balanced set, and the record says that deployment prevalence differs, which is exactly why every remove carries a probability and escalation remains the safeguard for everything between.

14.8 What the book claims about ethics, and what it does not

The ethics stack the instrument sits on keeps a concept registry [48] whose first rule is that every concept has one authoritative source and its second is that a posited claim may not be stated as fact anywhere. This section applies that rule to the chapter.

Measured. Eight of nine axes validated and one failed, with the numbers above. One axis discovered, gated at the identical bar, and admitted. Reliability weights and calibration errors per axis. Invariance ratios of 0.219 and 0.301 with their refusal and register caveats. A learned contraction at 0.863 on disjoint rows. The Klein four-group structure of two normative operations, a correlative swap and a negation, proved in a machine-checked Lean development.

Posited. The larger non-abelian group that the same stack's earlier papers headlined, which requires a quarter-turn operation no protocol has elicited, and which the registry marks as posited with a list of the documents that still state it as fact. The word quantum in a companion program's name, which a capability ladder falsified at every point tested [49], with signalling-corrected contextuality exactly zero in all 320 model and scenario probes. The four geometric volumes on cognition, reasoning, education, and alignment, which their own front pages mark as posited frameworks awaiting instruments.

A sidebar the same discipline produced. A study of whether the geometry of a text's embedding predicts its reader rating [31] found a discovery correlation of 0.241 on 4998 books that fell to 0.093 once genre was residualized, chapter 8's confound case. The residual is real at 6.5 standard errors and it is one to two percent of variance, and the record says both.

The book's last claim is the registry's rule stated as the course's closing habit. State the test before the evidence, and state a posited claim as posited wherever it appears.

14.9 What a deployment report must state

The final report's responsible-deployment section states the following.

  • The decision as a spectrum. Which values or criteria the decision reads, each with its validation record or a flag that it has none.
  • Authority per criterion, as a reliability weight from held-out AUROC, with calibration error beside it.
  • Coverage. What the model can read compared to what its inputs carry, with the noise floor, and any category that clears it named and dispositioned.
  • Invariance. The decision's movement across rewordings of the same input, with the refusal rate of the rewording generator and the adversarial case stated as met or unmet.
  • Fairness per group, as the worst group's metric with abstentions counted, the base rates, and the null.
  • Attributions named for what they measure, gradient-based ones as estimates of the consumer's sensitivity and the others as the averages or decompositions they are, with the interactions that no read-operator estimate can see acknowledged.
  • Monitoring as certificate and witness, with the per-slice false-clear rate and the escalation rule.
  • Posited versus measured, for every claim the report makes.

That an escalation bar on the margin can only lower coverage as it rises, and that coverage times the decided false-clear rate is the fraction of all items cleared and wrong, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

A report that asserts fairness, interpretability, or robustness without a number, a group, and a null will be returned.

Exercises

Warm-up

  1. Name the three questions a scalar toxicity score cannot answer and the instrument's mechanism for each.
  2. Why can an unvalidated encoder not vote, and where is that rule enforced?
  3. Explain why the binding null differed between axes in the re-gate table and what that changed.
  4. Why does a reliability weight temper authority but not transparency?
  5. Why was the escalation trigger left on raw confidence when the weights were introduced?
  6. State what the coverage-difference noise floor is and why a real missing dimension must clear it.
  7. Why was sexual content declined as a moral dimension while identity attack was admitted?
  8. Why does the record call the blind-recovery experiment procedurally rather than epistemically blind?
  9. State the difference between the invariance ratio and a flip rate, and which one the euphemism test measured.
  10. Why is an averaged gradient importance approximately a diagonal entry of the read operator, what do the off-diagonal entries measure, and why can neither see an interaction?
  11. State the impossibility result in the book's terms.
  12. Map drift alarms, shadow deployment, and retraining cadence onto chapter 13's three objects.
  13. Give one claim in this chapter that is measured and one that is posited, and say where each is recorded.

By hand

  1. Compute reliability weights, equation 0.38, for AUROC 0.63, 0.80, 0.853, and 0.50.
  2. Three axes score 0.9, 0.2, and 0.6 on an item with weights 0.71, 0.26, and 0.61. Compute the weighted contraction of equation 14.1 and the unweighted mean.
  3. A category reads AUROC 0.750 from the validated axes and 0.987 from the embedding. Compute the coverage difference and state whether it clears a floor of 0.12. Repeat for 0.878 and 0.971.
  4. Within-class decision movement averages 0.044 and between-input movement averages 0.199. Compute the invariance ratio and state whether the bar of 0.5 is met. Repeat for 0.079 and 0.199.
  5. A generator refuses 19 of 79 items. Compute the refusal rate. State the class size of a refused item and what the mechanism can do with it.
  6. Two groups have false-clear rates 0.04 and 0.31 and a third has 12 rows against a minimum of 50. Apply equation 14.5 and state the verdict and the abstention count.
  7. Two operations each undo themselves and commute. List the four elements of the group they generate and state whether it is the group of order 4 or the group of order 8.

Programming

  1. For your project's classifier, compute per-feature importances, a partial dependence curve for the top feature, and a per-row attribution for three rows. Compare each to the finite-difference read operator of section 0.5. Report where the gradient-based one agrees with it, and where the others answer a different question, remembering that the read operator's off-diagonal entries are co-sensitivities and not the interactions on which the methods disagree.
  2. Split your held-out set by a protected or proxy attribute into groups. Report per-group recall and false-clear rate, the worst group, the abstentions at a stated minimum, and the same metrics on shuffled group labels.
  3. Generate six rewordings of thirty held-out inputs by any available paraphraser. Report the refusal rate, then the invariance ratio of equation 14.3 with and without class averaging.
  4. Build a contraction of your model's component scores to its final decision with chapter 6's formula search and report whether a depth-three formula reproduces the decision.
  5. Split your deployment data into at least ten slices and report per-slice accuracy beside the benchmark score, with the naive false-clear rate at a stated target.
  6. Write the posited-versus-measured table for every claim in your final report, with the artifact behind each measured one.

Answers to the warm-up and by-hand problems are collected in the answer key at the end of the book.
