Chapter 13. Scale, cost, and the certificate that ages

Data Mining as Observation, draft 0.3, 2026-09-09. Week 13 of ECE 514. Reading due before this chapter is the instructor's notes on scale and cost and the case-study packet for the Wednesday session. Every number below traces to a committed file listed in the last section. Section 0.13 of the toolkit introduces the vocabulary this chapter adds.

Every chapter so far has assumed that a read, once made, stays true. This chapter adds a clock and a bill.

The bill first. At scale, cost is not an afterthought to a data-mining pipeline. It is the third element of the observer triple, the budget, and chapter 4 showed that a verdict changes when the budget does. Section 13.1 says what that means for a system measured in dollars per query.

Then the clock. A cache can be wrong in two ways. It can discard the structure its consumer needed, which is the failure the first twelve chapters were about. Or it can return an answer that was right and is no longer, because the world moved between the write and the read. No amount of the static theory sees the second failure, because the static theory has no clock. The rest of the chapter gives it one, and ends with the three preregistered attempts on a production serving stack that the Wednesday session walks through.

13.1 Cost is the budget in the triple

The session's Monday material covers out-of-core processing, sampling and sketches, and distributed patterns, and it makes one demand of every report. Dollars per query and dollars per training run are first-class metrics. This section adds four measured facts about what those numbers hide.

A number measured warm is not the number. A placement study for a sharded vector index reported search times [12] that were, in every prior report, taken with the operating system's file cache already holding the shards. A single cold measurement at eight probes read 22.30 seconds against 0.835 seconds warm. The record's own note is that every previously reported placement number was fully warm and must be labelled as such. A cost figure without the word warm or cold beside it is not a cost figure.

A speedup that was fragmentation. The same study found a speedup between two shard placements that appeared superlinear in the number of shards touched. It was not superlinear in anything causal. Both placements opened all 64 shards and scanned identical rows. The difference was file fragmentation, a ratio of 63.7 between the two layouts, bracketing the 42 to 47 times observed. Wall time tracked rows scanned, not shards touched. The mechanism named in the first report was wrong and the record says so.

A saving computed and discarded. The router in that system derived which shards each query needed and then opened every shard anyway. The routing statistic it reported was therefore not a transaction saving. Making the scan skip unneeded shards would convert it into one, and the record lists that as untested rather than as done.

A scale result that is not a retrieval result. The vector-search benchmark of chapter 11 built a corpus of one hundred billion vectors [16] on a preemptible fleet, regenerating shards from seeds with zero corpus movement. That validates distribution. Its retrieval numbers were measured on the same synthetic corpus, which the benchmark's own admission battery rejects as a stand-in for real data, so they are recorded as a systems result and not as a benchmark tier. A twelve-figure corpus is about 128 terabytes, and the benchmark distributes a signed manifest of a few kilobytes from which any shard can be regenerated and hash-verified. The scale is real. The retrieval claim waits.

That the global top k of a sharded index lies inside the union of the shards' top k, so the merge loses nothing and reads at most the shard count times k candidates, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The rule for a project is one sentence. A cost is reported at a named budget, with the cache state named, with the mechanism named only if it was tested, and separately from any quality claim measured on different data.

13.2 A certificate is a claim about a read at a time

Almost every operational decision rests not on a measurement but on a certificate [13, 14]. The medium is idle. The replica is fresh enough. This rate is supportable. The device is calibrated. The network has converged. Each licenses an action, and each is a claim about what the observer read at some time. A certificate ages. The state it describes decorrelates over its coherence time, and a certificate issued at one time and acted on later can be wrong for no reason other than the delay. The static theory of the earlier chapters is the zero-delay slice, and zero delay is instantaneous certainty pretending to be the normal case.

Freshness needs three objects, and the discipline is in taking each literally.

The certificate is the asserted-safe read at a time.

The witness is an independent measurement of the true outcome, native to the system. A claim you cannot grade is not a claim, and the witnesses are already deployed.

The refresh interval is the longest renewal period that keeps the certificate within its promised error. It is not a convention. It is a measured property of the system, and section 13.4 measures it.

The one number the rest of the chapter is built on is the false-clear rate, the probability that the witness refutes a clearance, conditional on the certificate having cleared. A certificate whose false-clear rate exceeds the target it purports to guarantee is vacuous for that consumer. Chapter 8 used the word vacuous for a pass that never met its event. This is the same word with a clock.

$$\mathrm{FC}=\Pr\big[W\ \text{refutes}\ \big|\ \mathcal C_t\ \text{clears}\big],\qquad d_O(\Delta)=\operatorname{tr}\big(P_C\,M_{\mathrm{drift}}(\Delta)\big),\qquad M_{\mathrm{drift}}(\Delta)=\mathbb E\big[\delta_\Delta\delta_\Delta^{\top}\big]. \tag{13.1}$$

That the same drift is read as its full squared size by one consumer and as nothing by another, and as zero by any consumer whose nuisance contains it, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

That the false-clear rate times the coverage is the joint rate of clearing and being refuted is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

Every false-clear rate in this chapter is conditional on the certificate having cleared, as equation 0.26 defines it, which is also how the freshness program defines the rate, as how often a clearance was wrong. A naive certificate clears every decision, so its coverage is one and its conditional and joint rates coincide. A witnessed certificate clears fewer, and its rate is read beside its coverage.

Chapter 4 wrote the consumer's distortion as the trace of the read operator times the error's second-moment matrix. Freshness reads the same expression with a different error. The second-moment matrix is now that of the drift between issue and use, and the trace is the consumer-felt cost of acting on a stale read. Same operator. The clock supplies the error. That is why freshness is a face of the theory rather than an appendix to it.

13.3 Staleness is consumer-relative

The first measured fact about the false-clear rate is that it is not a property of the certificate. It is a property of the certificate and the consumer together.

Two readers of one replica. [14] A ZooKeeper follower was served under write load and graded against its transaction identifier as witness. A client whose reads concentrated on a hot footprint, the keys being written, was stale on 99 percent of reads. A client reading a cold footprint on the same follower at the same instant was stale on 1 percent. The same lag bound cleared both. A witnessed read, gated on the transaction identifier, brought both to zero. There is no single statement of the form "the replica is fresh enough" that serves both readers, because the aggregate lag bound is the identity reader of chapter 4 wearing a clock.

The same shape across replication systems. A Postgres replica with a delayed apply read a naive false-clear rate near 0.50 against its write-ahead position as witness, and about 0.06 witnessed. A MongoDB delayed secondary read about 0.47 naive and 0.03 witnessed. A production Postgres under emulated network lag read about 0.47 and 0.02. Each cell was sealed, run on a real substrate rather than a simulator, and graded on seeds disjoint from its calibration.

Routing. A converged-routing certificate, the claim that the network's routes have settled, was graded against a data-plane witness on three protocols. The naive certificate false-cleared at 0.351 for BGP, 0.184 for IS-IS, and 0.083 for OSPF. A witnessed detector brought each into a registered band. The rows are replicated across a second collector.

Radio. A channel-state report in a 5G link ages over the channel's coherence time and is graded by the receiver's acknowledgements. Across the physical-layer cells, naive false-clear rates of 0.27 to 0.42 fell to 0.055 to 0.13 with a witnessed correction. One cell used a neural channel reconstruction of the kind chapter 11 would certify by reconstruction error, and it won on reconstruction while its false-clear rate went from 0.28 to 0.13 only under the witness. The reconstruction metric could not see the staleness.

That two readers with different coherence times disagree about every age between the two, and that a mixed population's staleness rate lies strictly between the two readers' rates and so describes neither, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

That a naive certificate clearing every read has a false-clear rate equal to the stale fraction, and that one clearing only keys outside the lag has a false-clear rate of zero at the price of coverage, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

Across these rows the word that recurs is footprint. What the reader reads decides how stale the reader is. The chapter 3 sentence about hubs, that a point is a hub because of what the queries ask for, has a temporal twin. A replica is stale because of what the reader asks for.

13.4 The refresh interval has no proportionality law, and the law that said it did was refuted

The intuitive claim is that the refresh interval scales with the coherence time. Measure how fast the channel decorrelates, and refresh at a fixed fraction of that. An exploratory sweep on a simulated 5G link found exactly that, a floor near 0.177 times the coherence time with an R² of 0.915, and the number went into a conference draft.

$$\begin{gathered} T_{\mathrm{coh}}=\frac{0.423}{f_D}, \\ \text{refuted fit:}\ \ \phi\approx0.177\,T_{\mathrm{coh}}\ (R^{2}=0.915), \qquad \text{sealed:}\ \ \phi\le 6\ \text{TTI at }10\ \text{Hz},\ \ 1\ \text{TTI at}\ \ge50\ \text{Hz}. \end{gathered} \tag{13.2}$$

That a block of independently lost symbols fails with probability at most the symbol count times the loss rate, and no less often as the block grows, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The arithmetic of the refuted law against the sealed floors is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C. The refutation rests on the sealed record.

An external reviewer of that draft found two defects. The sweep had measured its floors at a relaxed block-error threshold of 0.15 while the paper claimed a target of 0.10. And the fresh baseline, the error rate with no aging at all, was 0.113, which never met the claimed budget in the first place. The law had been fit to a horizon that existed only because the bar was lower than stated.

The correction was sealed as a new cell, constructed on one day and sealed the next under the rate-limit rule of chapter 8. It calibrated the fresh baseline to the budget first, a backoff of half a decibel, floored at the budget itself, and censored rather than clamped when no report period complied. Three graded seeds, disjoint from the pilots and from the calibration seed.

At the true budget the horizon collapses. The origin-fit slopes on the three seeds were 0.101, 0.140, and 0.109, each below the sealed bar of 0.14 and all below the exploration's 0.177, with R² of 0.74, 0.92, and 0.62. There is almost no horizon left to scale, so there is no proportionality law to fit. All four bars and four manipulation checks passed on every seed. The operational reading is that periodic reporting alone cannot protect a tight budget at any practical mobility, and the witnessed correction is mandatory rather than optional.

The refuted record is kept with a note that says do not cite. The chapter cites it here for one reason. It is the chapter 8 lesson in the chapter's own domain. A fit with R² of 0.915 is not a law when the bar it was fit against is not the bar that was claimed.

That a duration of $m$ milliseconds spans $m$ transmission time intervals, and that the slots add and grow with the duration, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The other domains in the freshness sweep are carried at their earned classes. Routing and replication are replicated. Radio is demonstrated. Optical networks and the power grid are predicted with seals pending. Quantum devices and markets are exploratory, gated on hardware and data the program does not have. One row is a kept negative. On real field sensor data the drift was real and a linear recalibration recovered only about 1.6 times, so the sensing certificate is carried as refuted.

13.5 Relevance lost to time is paid for as work

There is a physical reading of staleness, and the program tested it under seal because it makes a prediction the information-theoretic reading does not.

Landauer's principle, introduced in section 0.13, says erasing a bit costs energy [46, 47]. Erasing is cheaper when a second record still holds part of what is being erased, because less has to be forgotten. A stored description of a data source therefore has two separate prices. The rate needed to describe it, in bits per symbol, and the work needed to reset it given whatever else the system remembers.

$$W_{\mathrm{reset}}\ \ge\ k_BT\ln2\ \cdot\ H(M\mid S),\qquad H(M\mid S)\le H(M). \tag{13.3}$$

The two prices are separate. A code index needing about 0.67 bits per symbol to describe was fully recoverable from retained side information at a bin rate of 0.26, which is 0.39 of its description rate. Below its conditional content, recovery failed increasingly. Without the side information it failed absolutely, error 1.00 at every bin rate. The result held on two independent source families and six codebooks, and it is the operational form of the statement that what the system already knows reduces what erasure costs.

A stale memory helps less. The same construction was run with the side information aged. On a Markov source with flip probability 0.05, the decodable bin rate rose from 0.10 to 0.55 bits per symbol as the side information aged from 0 to 64 steps, tracking the predicted discount within one grid step at every age. A fixed bin rate flipped from 1 percent error at age 0 to 100 percent at age 32. The Gaussian replication passed all five bars, and its ledger row carries a caveat at full prominence. The control statistic as executed had a bug on single-codeword bins, the as-executed record is committed unchanged, the rerun used the same seeds, and the experimental data is bit-identical.

That a cache's hit rate is zero cold and one warm, never falls when the cache holds more, and is unchanged by entries outside the requests, so that the footprint is the requests' property, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

For a data-mining system the reading is direct. A cache entry, a materialized view, a precomputed embedding, each is a record whose value to the consumer decays with the age of what it was computed from. The decay is not a nuisance. It is a price, and it can be measured.

13.6 Three preregistered attempts on a production serving stack

The Wednesday session walks the case-study packet [13], and this section is its written form. The subject is the key-value cache of a seven-billion-parameter language model, section 0.11, serving passage retrieval over contexts of about fourteen thousand tokens. Three registrations, one registered miss, one identifier burned at pilot, one split verdict.

Attempt one, the miss. The claim was chapter 4's flip on a live serving path. Perturb the stored keys as the model generates, with two arms at identical bits and identical reconstruction error, one placing the error outside the read subspace and one inside it, plus a control that places it inside a different head's subspace. The mechanism had already been shown on captured activations, where in-subspace error raised the attention divergence 15 to 36 times over complement error in every head tested. The prereg sealed six gates, and the governed run on 40 prompts passed four.

Reconstruction ratio was 1.000000 on every arm, so the arms were genuinely matched. The primary gate required the preserve arm to beat the destroy arm by 0.15 and measured 0.075. The specificity gate required 0.10 and measured 0.050. Both were half their bars. Per the sealed falsification clause, the operational claim was refuted at its registered effect sizes on this model and task. No bar was moved. No second attempt was registered under the same identifier. The power note written before the verdict had said what effect size the run could resolve.

Attempt two, killed at pilot. The successor reframed the cache as records, the rolling context as an aging reference, and eviction as erasure, and proposed a score based on cumulative attention history as the consumer proxy. The pilot found that the proxy was broken and that the equal-uncertainty control the design needed did not exist as a constructible object. The identifier was burned with the design failure disclosed in the draft and the pilot record committed. That is a cheap success. It cost sixteen prompts and twenty-three minutes and prevented a governed run on a broken instrument.

Attempt three, the split verdict. The redesign made every scorer a member of one consumer-relative family, the predicted future attention mass estimated from a window of recent queries, with window widths of 1024, 256, and 32. The pilot's central measurement stood. On an aging reference, predictive uncertainty about the consumer's future reads improved as the window narrowed, from 0.982 with the full history to 0.892 with the 32-query snapshot, because unweighted wide access includes staler evidence. The sealed prereg made two predictions. First, at matched budget, decode quality across the three windows is ordered by measured uncertainty, not by nominal width. Second, a snapshot scorer degraded with calibrated noise until its uncertainty equals the 256-query scorer's will give equal quality, because the theory says access class enters quality only through that one number.

The governed run, 64 fresh prompts, seed 20260812, passed five of six gates. The 32-query snapshot beat the 1024-query scorer by 0.4375 with standard error 0.070 at 5 percent keep, against a bar of 0.30. It survived 97 percent eviction with zero drop. Wide-window scoring at extreme budgets was statistically indistinguishable from random eviction. The recency-hoarding starvation signature replicated across three disjoint prompt sets, with the oracle-miss difference at 0.370 against a bar of 0.25. The first prediction is demonstrated.

The second prediction failed on its own pre-registered branch. With calibration health four to 130 times inside its gates, equal scalar uncertainty did not give equal quality. The difference was 0.078 against a tolerance of 0.0625, and the contrast the prereg had estimated at 0.03 firmed to 0.359 with standard error 0.068, more than five standard errors. Equal scalar uncertainty is insufficient. The structure of the error matters, which is what the program's own theorem had said for records of rank two and above. The refutation was pre-registered as a finding, and it is carried as refuted beside the demonstrated half. The record discloses 89 thermal-guardian duty cycles during the run.

That attention over the kept tokens lies between their values and equals them when they agree, so that which tokens can go is a question about the reader and not the cache, is machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.

The post-mortem discipline. The Wednesday session asks students to reconstruct the story that would have been told without the preregistration. It is easy to write. Attempt one's 0.075 becomes a positive trend. Attempt two's broken proxy is never mentioned. Attempt three's refuted half is dropped and the demonstrated half is the headline. Three registrations and two kept negatives are what stand between that story and the record.

13.7 What a deployment report must state

A project that deploys, caches, replicates, or serves states the following beside its performance numbers.

  • The budget. Dollars, seconds, or bits per query, at a named cache state, with cold and warm both reported if the system has a cache.
  • The certificate and its witness. What claim the system acts on, what independent measurement grades it, and the false-clear rate per consumer footprint, not in aggregate.
  • The refresh interval, measured. The longest period at which the certificate stayed within its promised error on held-out data, with the threshold stated and the fresh baseline shown to meet it.
  • The mechanism, if named. A speedup or a saving attributed to a cause only if the cause was tested. Otherwise the number stands alone.
  • The preregistration. The bar, the commit hash, and the verdict, with any refuted half carried at the prominence of the demonstrated one.

A report that gives a warm number without saying so, or a false-clear rate averaged over readers with different footprints, will be returned.

Exercises

Warm-up

  1. State the three elements of the observer triple as they appear in a cost report, and say which one this chapter is about.
  2. Why is a placement number measured warm not comparable to one measured cold?
  3. Explain the difference between a certificate and a witness, using an example not in the chapter's table.
  4. Why is the false-clear rate the right number rather than the certificate's own confidence?
  5. Two readers of one replica have false-clear rates of 0.99 and 0.01. What does the aggregate rate tell either of them?
  6. State the two defects the external reviewer found in the refresh-floor sweep, and say which of chapter 8's rules each violates.
  7. Why did the sealed correction censor rather than clamp when no report period complied?
  8. Explain in one sentence why a stale side record makes erasure more expensive.
  9. Attempt one measured 0.075 against a bar of 0.15. Write the sentence a report without a preregistration would have written, then write the sentence the record wrote.
  10. Why was burning attempt two's identifier at pilot a success?
  11. Attempt three passed five of six gates. State what the failed gate refuted and why it is carried beside the passes.
  12. Name the reason the one-hundred-billion-vector build is not a benchmark tier.

By hand

  1. Using Clarke's formula, compute the coherence time at Doppler frequencies of 10, 50, and 400 hertz. With a one-millisecond TTI, express the sealed floors of 4, 1, and 1 TTI as fractions of the coherence time. State what floor the refuted law would have predicted at 10 hertz.
  2. A replica serves 1000 reads. Two hundred are on a hot footprint, of which 198 return stale versions. Eight hundred are on a cold footprint, of which 8 are stale. Compute the false-clear rate for each reader and the aggregate rate, and state which reader the aggregate describes.
  3. Compute Landauer's minimum energy per erased bit at 300 kelvin, using Boltzmann's constant 1.38 times 10 to the negative 23 joules per kelvin. Compute the minimum energy to erase one billion bits.
  4. A cache of 14000 entries is evicted to 5 percent keep. Compute the number of entries retained. Compute it again at 97 percent eviction.
  5. A code needs 0.67 bits per symbol to describe and is recoverable from side information at a bin rate of 0.26. Compute the fraction of the description rate the side information saved.
  6. A search read 22.30 seconds cold and 0.835 seconds warm. Compute the ratio. A report quotes the warm figure as the system's latency. State the factor by which a first-time user's experience differs from the report.
  7. A corpus of one trillion vectors at 128 bytes each is distributed as a manifest of 4 kilobytes. Compute the corpus size in terabytes and the ratio between corpus and manifest.

Programming

  1. Simulate a value with a known coherence time as a first-order autoregressive process. Issue a certificate at each step that the value is within a tolerance, act on it after a delay, and grade it against the true value. Plot the false-clear rate against delay in units of the coherence time.
  2. Repeat exercise 20 with two consumers that read different tolerances of the same value. Report the two false-clear rates at a fixed delay and the aggregate rate.
  3. For a database or cache in your project, identify the certificate the system acts on and a witness that grades it. Measure the false-clear rate under a write load for a hot footprint and a cold one.
  4. Sweep the refresh period of exercise 20's certificate and report the largest period at which the false-clear rate stays under a stated budget. Calibrate the fresh baseline to the budget first, then repeat at a relaxed budget, and report both horizons.
  5. Time one query pipeline in your project cold and warm, five trials each with the warm-up discarded. Report minimum, median, and maximum for both.
  6. Reproduce attempt one's design on any open model with a patchable cache. Place a fixed-norm error inside and outside a head's read subspace, hold reconstruction ratio at one, and report the task score for each arm on twenty prompts. State your bar before running.
  7. Write the preregistration for one freshness claim in your project using the eight fields of chapter 8. Commit it. Run the measurement and report the verdict against the bar.

Answers to the warm-up and by-hand problems are collected in the answer key at the end of the book.
